Throughout this course, ``ring'' means \textit{commutative} ring with unity and all ring homomorphisms preserve unity. In the previous worksheets we have repeatedly moved between rings, modules, quotients, localization, and spectra. The goal of this worksheet is to introduce a language that lets us treat many ring maps as geometric families: the language of \emph{$R$-algebras}. Once this language is in place, we will distinguish two finiteness notions that sound similar but behave very differently: finite generation as an algebra and finite generation as a module.

The main bridge between these two notions is integrality. Integral elements are elements of an extension ring that satisfy monic polynomial equations over the base. The monic condition is what lets one convert algebraic equations into module-theoretic finiteness. This is one of the basic mechanisms behind many finiteness theorems in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry.


Let $R$ be a ring. An \emph{$R$-algebra} is a ring $S$ together with a specified ring homomorphism $\alpha:R\longrightarrow S$. Note we do not require that $\alpha$ be injective, however, the case when $\alpha$ is an inclusion, i.e., $\K$ into $\K[x]$, is generally the motivating example. We often think of an $R$-algebra structure on a ring $S$ as giving us a way to multiply elements of $S$ by elements of $R$, i.e., $r \cdot s \coloneqq \alpha(r)s$. Thus every $R$-algebra $S$ is naturally an $R$-module. The key difference between an $R$-module $M$ and an $R$-algebra $S$ is that for module we may only multiple elements of $M$ by elements of $R$, but for an $R$-algebra we every element of $S$ may be multiplied together. The key analogy might be: $R$-modules are to vector spaces, as $R$-algebras are to rings. 

An $R$-algebra homomorphism $\phi:S\to T$ is a ring homomorphism compatible with the structure maps from $R$, meaning that the diagram
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=4em, column sep=4em]
S \arrow[rr, "\phi"] \arrow[dr, "\alpha_{S}"]& & T \arrow[dl, "\alpha_{T}"] \\
& T &
\end{tikzcd}
\]
commutes. Equivalently, $\phi(\alpha_S(r))=\alpha_{T}(r)$ for every $r\in R$. An \emph{$R$-subalgebra} of $S$ is a subring $S' \subset S$, which contains $\alpha(R)$. Given any set $\cS \subset S$ the \emph{$R$-algebra generated by $\cS$} is the smallest $R$-subalgebra of $S$ containing $\cS$. We often write $R[\cS]$ to denote $R$-subalgebra generated by $\cS$, because it can explicitly be written down as the set of polynomials in the elements in $\cS$ with coefficients in $R$.

\begin{problem}{1}
    \textbf{First Encounters with $R$-algebras.} 
    
    Let $\alpha:R\to S$ be an $R$-algebra.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Verify that the rule $r\cdot s=\alpha(r)s$ makes $S$ into an $R$-module.
        \item Give an example where $\alpha$ is not injective. In your example, identify a nonzero element of $R$ that acts as zero on $S$.
        \item Show that every quotient $R/I$ is naturally an $R$-algebra.
        \item Show that if $S$ and $T$ are $R$-algebras and $\phi:S\to T$ is an $R$-algebra homomorphism, then $\phi$ is $R$-linear for the underlying $R$-module structures.
        \item Let $\K \subseteq \E$ be a field extension. Explain why $\E$ is a $\K$-algebra. What is the underlying $\K$-module structure?
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item We verify the axioms. For all $r, p \in R, s, t \in S$ we have: 
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item $r(s + t) = \alpha(r) (s+t) = \alpha(r) s + \alpha(r)t = rs + rt$
            \item $(r + p) t = \alpha(r + p) t = \alpha(r) t + \alpha(p)t = rt + pt$
            \item $(rp)t = \alpha(rp) t = \alpha(r) \alpha(p) t =\alpha(r) (\alpha(p) t) = r(pt)$
        \end{enumerate}
        Thus the rule $r \cdot s = \alpha(r) s$ makes $S$ into a $R$-module. 
        
        \item $\alpha: \Z \to \Z/2\Z$. 
        
        $2$ is a non-zero element in $\Z$ but acts as zero on $\Z/2\Z$.

        \item The natural projection $\pi: R \to R/I$ is a ring homomorphism, and the property we proved in part (1) works with substituting $\alpha = \pi$. 
        
        \item Let $\alpha: R \to S$ and $\beta: R \to T$. Then, for $r, s \in R$, 
        \[\phi(r \cdot s) = \phi(\alpha(r)s) = \phi(\alpha(r))\phi(s) = \beta(r)\phi(s)  =r \phi(s)\]
        Hence, $\phi$ is $R$-linear. 

        \item $\E$ is a $\K$-algebra with the inclusion map $\iota: \K \to \E, \, \iota(k) = k$ as the ring homomorphism. Moreover multiplication in $\E$ is $\K$-linear and, 
        \[k \cdot e = \iota(k)e = k e\]
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{2}
    \textbf{Polynomial Rings and Generation of $R$-algebras.} 
    
    Let $S$ be an $R$-algebra and let $s_1,\ldots,s_n\in S$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Construct an $R$-algebra homomorphism
        \[
        \ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}:R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\longrightarrow S,
        \qquad
        f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\longmapsto f(s_1,\ldots,s_n).
        \]
        \item Show that the image of this map is the smallest $R$-subalgebra of $S$ containing $s_1,\ldots,s_n$. We denote this image by $R[s_1,\ldots,s_n]$.
        \item Prove that $S=R[s_1,\ldots,s_n]$ if and only if $\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}$ is surjective.
        \item Deduce that $S$ is generated as an $R$-algebra by $n$ elements if and only if $S\cong R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I$ as an $R$-algebra for some ideal $I \subset R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item We define $\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}:R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\longrightarrow S$ by, 
        \[\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}(f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)) = \ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}\left(\sum_i r_i x_i\right) = f(s_1, \ldots, s_n) = \sum_i r_i(s_i) \in S\]

        This is a homomorphism since both 
        \[(f+g)(s_1, \ldots, s_n) = \sum_i (r_i + r'_i) s_i = \sum_i r_i s_i + \sum_i r_i' s_i = f(s_1, \ldots, s_n) + g(s_1, \ldots, s_n)\] 
        and,
        \[(fg)(s_1, \ldots, s_n) = \sum_i (r_i \cdot r'_i) s_i = f(s_1, \ldots, s_n) \cdot g(s_1, \ldots, s_n)\] 
        hold.

        \item We know that $\im(\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}) = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$. Let $T \subseteq S$ be a $R$-subalgebra with $s_1, \ldots, s_n \in T$. Note that a general element of $R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$ has the form 
        \[f(s_1, \ldots, s_n) = \sum_i r_i s_1^{i_1} \cdots s_n^{i_n}\]
        But since $T$ is a $R$-subalgebra the sum $ \sum_i r_i s_1^{i_1} \cdots s_n^{i_n} \in T$ since $T$ is closed under addition and multiplication and $r_i \in T$ for all $r_i \in R$. Thus, $R[s_1, \ldots, s_n] \subseteq T$. Since $R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$ is contained in every $R$-subalgebra of $S$ containing $s_1, \ldots, s_n$ and is itself such a subalgebra it must be the smallest one. 

        \item We know that $\im(\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}) = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n] \subseteq S$. If 
        $S = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$ then 
        \[S  = \im(\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n})\]
        and $\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}$ are surjective. 

        \item $(\implies)$ 
        
        Assume $S$ is a $R$-algebra generated by $s_1, \ldots, s_n \in S$. Then we have $S = R[x_1, \ldots, x_n] = \im(\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n})$ by part 3. Then by the first isomorphism theorem, 
        \[S = \im(\ev_{s_1,\ldots,s_n}) = R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I\]
        for some $I \subseteq R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. 

        $(\impliedby)$

        Assume $S \iso R[x_1, \ldots, x_n] / I$. Then there exists a isomorphism $\phi: R[x_1, \ldots, x_n] / I \to S$, where $s_i = \phi(x_i + I)$ for $1 \leq i \leq n$. Then every element of $S$ can be written as 

        \[\phi(f + I) = \phi\left(\sum_i r_ix_1^{i_1} \cdots x_n^{i_n} + I\right) = r_is_1^{i_1} \cdots s_n^{i_n} = f(s_1, \ldots, s_n)\]

        so every element of $S$ is a polynomial expression in $s_1, \ldots, s_n$ with coefficients in $R$. Hence $S = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$ is an $R$-algebra generated by $n$ elements. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

As discussed above an $R$-algebra $S$ is also always an $R$-module with the same $R$-multiplication. This leads to two different finiteness conditions depending on whether we wish to view $S$ as an $R$-module or as an $R$-algebra. Let $S$ be an $R$-algebra.

\begin{definition}
   We say that $S$ is \emph{algebra-finite over $R$} if $S$ is finitely generated s as an $R$-algebra.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	We say that $S$ is \emph{module-finite over $R$} if $S$ is finitely generated as an $R$-module.
\end{definition}

The condition $S$ be algebra-finite over $R$ is sometimes called being \emph{finite type} over $R$, i.e. we would say $S$ is finite type over $R$ if and only if $S$ is algebra-finite over $R$. Similarly, many authors will use the phrase $S$ is \emph{finite} over $R$ or $S$ is a \emph{finite $R$-algebra} to mean $S$ if a $R$-algebra that is module-finite,. In this worksheet we will attempt to restrict to using \emph{algebra-finite} and \emph{module-finite} to hopefully minimize confusion.

The key point is that module-finite is much stronger than algebra-finite. A module-finite algebra is controlled by finitely many $R$-linear combinations. An algebra-finite algebra is controlled by finitely many elements and all polynomial expressions in them.

\begin{problem}{4}
    \textbf{Module-Finite Implies Algebra-Finite.} 
    
    Let $S$ be an $R$-algebra and suppose that $S$ is generated as an $R$-module by $m_1,\ldots,m_n\in S$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Show that the $R$-subalgebra $R[m_1,\ldots,m_n]$ contains every $R$-linear combination of $m_1,\ldots,m_n$.
        \item Deduce that $S=R[m_1,\ldots,m_n]$.
        \item Conclude that every module-finite $R$-algebra is algebra-finite over $R$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item An $R$-linear combination is of the form $r_1m_1 + \cdots r_nm_n$ for all $r_i \in R$. But this is the polynomial $r_1x_1 + \cdots r_nx_n$  evaluated at $m_1, \ldots, m_n$ which is an element of $\im(ev_{m_1, \ldots, m_n}) = R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$.
        
        \item The containment $S \supseteq R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$ is obvious because the latter is a subalgebra of $S$ and part 1 gives us the reverse containment, hence we have $S = R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$. 
        
        \item Thus every module-finite $R$-algebra generated by $n$ elements can be written in the form $R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$ (by part 2) and thus is algebra-finite over $R$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{5} \textbf{Algebra-Finite Need Not Imply Module-Finite.} 
    
    Assume $R$ is nonzero.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Show that $R[x]$ is algebra-finite over $R$.
        \item Suppose $p_1,\ldots,p_n\in R[x]$. Let $d$ be the maximum of their degrees. Show that every $R$-linear combination of the $p_i$ has degree at most $d$, unless it is zero.
        \item Deduce that $R[x]$ is not module-finite over $R$. Hint: Is $x^{d+1}$ in the $R$-span of $p_1,\ldots,p_n$?
        \item Specialize to $R=\K$ a field and restate the result in the language of vector spaces.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $R[x]$ is generated by $\{x\}$ as an algebra and hence is algebra-finite. 
        
        \item Every $R$-linear combination of the $p_i$ is of the form 
        \[r_1 p_1 + \cdots + r_np_n,  \quad r_i \in R \]
        since $\deg(r_i) = 0$ the degree of the equation is the maximum of the degrees of the $p_i$ which is $d$. 

        \item Assume $R[x]$ is module-finite over $R$. Then there exists a finite set of generating elements $p_1, \ldots, p_n \in R[x]$. Let $d$ be the maximum of their degrees. Now, $x^{d+1} \in R[x]$ with degree $d+1$ but by part 2 we know that any $R$ linear combination of the $p_i$ has degree at most $d$ --- a contradition. Hence, $R[x]$ is not module finite over $R$. 
        
        \item If $R = \K$ then $R[x]$ is an infinite dimensional vector space. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{6} \textbf{Permanence of Algebra-Finiteness.} 
    
    The goal of this exercise is to explore how being algebra-finite plays with many natural algebraic operations: quotienting, tensoring, localizing, and composing the underlying morphisms giving the algebra structure. Consider maps of rings:
    \[
    \begin{tikzcd}[row sep = .3em, column sep = 4em]
    R \arrow[r, "\alpha"] & S \arrow[r, "\beta"] & T
    \end{tikzcd}.
    \]
    The map $\alpha$ gives $S$ an $R$-algebra structure. On the other hand $T$ has two different algebra structures: we may think of $T$ as an $S$-algebra via the map $\beta$ or think of $T$ as an $R$-algebra via $\beta\circ\alpha$. 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If $S$ is algebra-finite over $R$ and $J\subseteq S$ is an ideal, prove that $S/J$ is algebra-finite over $R$.
        \item If $S$ is algebra-finite over $R$ and $T$ is algebra-finite over $S$, prove that $T$ is algebra-finite over $R$.
        \item Give an example where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are injective and $T$ is algebra-finite over $R$, but $S$ is not algebra-finite over $R$. Hint: Let $R=\K$, and $T = \K[x,y]$, what happens if $S=\K[x,xy,xy^{2},xy^{3},\ldots]$?
        \item If $S$ is algebra-finite over $R$, and $R\to T$ is a ring map, prove that $T\otimes_R S$ is algebra-finite over $T$. What are algebra generators?
        \item If $U\subset R$ is multiplicatively closed and $S$ is algebra-finite over $R$, prove that $U^{-1}S$ is algebra-finite over $U^{-1}R$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni 

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Let $S = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$. Then the quotinent map $\pi: S \to S/J$ is a surjective homomorphism with $\im(\pi) = R[\overline{s_1}, \ldots, \overline{s_n}]$, where $\pi(s_i) = \overline{s_i}$. Hence $S/J$ is algebra-finite over $R$. 
        
        \item Let $S = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$, and $T = S[t_1, \ldots, t_m]$. Then every element of $T$ is a polynomial in $t_1, \ldots, t_m$ with coefficients in $S$, and each coefficient is a polynomial in $s_1, \ldots, s_n$ with coefficients in $R$. Hence every element of $T$ is a polynomials expression in $\beta(s_1), \ldots, \beta(s_n), t_1, \ldots, t_m$ with coefficients in $R$, so
        \[T = R[\beta(s_1), \ldots, \beta(s_n), t_1, \ldots, t_m]\]
        which is algebra finite over $R$. 

        \item We let $R = \K$, $T = \K[x, y]$ and $S = R[x, xy, xy^2, xy^3, \ldots]$.
        
        \item Let $S = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$. Every element of $T \otimes_R S$ is a finite sum of simple tensors $t \otimes s$. Since $s \in S$, we can write $s = \sum_i r_i s_1^{i_1} \ldots s_n^{i_n}$ for $r_i \in R$. Then, 
        \begin{align*}
            t \otimes s &= t \otimes \sum_i r_i s_1^{i_1} \cdots s_n^{i_n} \\ 
            &= t \sum_i r_i \otimes \sum_i s_1^{i_1} \cdots s_n^{i_n}  \\ 
            &= \sum_i t r_i \otimes s_1^{i_1} \cdots s_n^{i_n} \\ 
            &= \sum_i t r_i  (1 \otimes s_1)^{i_1} \cdots (1 \otimes s_n)^{i_n} \\ 
        \end{align*}
        Which is a polynomial in $(1 \otimes s_1), \ldots, (1 \otimes s_n)$ with coefficients in $T$ and thus, these are the algebraic generators of $T\otimes_R S$ and  $T \otimes_R S = T [1 \otimes s_1, \ldots, 1 \otimes s_n]$. 


        \item Let $S = R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$. Note that the map $\eta: S \to U^{-1}S$ is a $R$-algebra homomorphism, and hence a $U^{-1}R$ algebra homomorphism (by extending scalars). Every element of $U^{-1}S$ is of the form $\frac{s}{u} = \frac{1}{u} \cdot \frac{s}{1}$ where $\frac{1}{u} \in U^{-1}R$. 
        
        Since every element $s \in S$ is of the form $s = \sum_i r_i s_1^{i_1} \cdots s_n^{i_n}$ we get 
        \[\frac{s}{u} = \frac{1}{u}\sum_i \frac{r_i}{1} \fr{s_1}{1}^{i_1} \cdots \fr{s_n}{1}^{i_n}\]
        
        
        which is a polynomial in $\frac{s_1}{1}, \ldots, \frac{s_n}{1}$.  So, $U^{-1}S = U^{-1}R[s_1, \ldots, s_n]$ which is algebra-finite over $R$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{7}
    \textbf{Permanence of Module-Finiteness.} 
    
    The goal of this exercise is to explore how being module-finite plays with many natural algebraic operations: quotienting, tensoring, localizing, and composing the underlying morphisms giving the algebra structure. Consider maps of rings:
    \[
    \begin{tikzcd}[row sep = .3em, column sep = 4em]
    R \arrow[r, "\alpha"] & S \arrow[r, "\beta"] & T
    \end{tikzcd}.
    \]
    The map $\alpha$ gives $S$ an $R$-module structure. On the other hand $T$ has two different module structures: we may think of $T$ as an $S$-module via the map $\beta$ or think of $T$ as an $R$-module via $\beta\circ\alpha$. 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If $S$ is module-finite over $R$ and $J\subseteq S$ is an ideal, prove that $S/J$ is module-finite over $R$.
        \item If $S$ is module-finite over $R$ and $T$ is module-finite over $S$, prove that $T$ is module-finite over $R$.
        \item If $S$ is module-finite over $R$, and $R\to T$ is a ring map, prove that $T\otimes_R S$ is module-finite over $T$.
        \item If $U\subset R$ is multiplicatively closed and $S$ is module-finite over $R$, prove that $U^{-1}S$ is module-finite over $U^{-1}R$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Let $S = R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$. Then, every element in $s \in S$ can be written as $s = \sum_i r_i m_i$ for $r_i \in R, \, m_i \in S$. Then, every element of $S/J$ can be written as $\overline{s} = \sum_i \overline{r_i} \, \overline{m_i} = \sum_i r_i \overline{m_i}$. Thus $S/J$ is spanned by the $\overline{m_i}$ as a $R$-module and hence is module-finite. 
        
        \item Let $T = S[n_1, \ldots, n_m]$ and $S = R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$. Then every element in $T$ can be written as $T = \sum_i s_i n_i$ with $s_i \in S$, and every $s_i$ can be written as $\sum_j r_jm_j$. Then every element in $T$ can be written as 
        \[t = \sum_i \left (\sum_j r_{ij} m_j \right ) n_i  = \sum_{i, j} r_{ij} (m_j n_i)\]
        Thus $T$ is spanned by the $m_jn_i$ as a $R$-module and hence is module-finite. 

        \item Let $S = R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$. Every element of $T \otimes_R S$ is a finite sum of simple tensors $t \otimes s$. Since $s \in S$, we can write $s = \sum_i r_i m_i$ for $r_i \in R$. Then, 
        \begin{align*}
            t \otimes s &= t \otimes \sum_i r_im_i \\ 
            &= t \sum_i r_i \otimes \sum_i m_i \\ 
            &= \sum_i tr_i \otimes m_i \\ 
            &= \sum_i tr_i (1 \otimes m_i) \\ 
        \end{align*}
        Thus, $T \otimes_R S$ is spanned by the $(1 \otimes m_i)$ and hence is module-finite. 

        \item Let $S = R[m_1, \ldots, m_n]$. Every element of $U^{-1}S$ is of the form, 
        \[\frac{s}{u} = \frac{1}{u} \cdot \frac{s}{1}\]
        with $\frac{1}{u} \in U^{-1}R$ and since every $s \in S$ is of the form $s = \sum_i r_i m_i$ and then we have, 
        \[\frac{s}{1} = \sum_i \frac{r_i}{1} \cdot \frac{m_i}{1}\]
        Thus, $U^{-1}S$ is spanned by $\frac{m_i}{1}$ as a $U^{-1}R$-module. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

Let $S$ be an $R$-algebra and let $s\in S$. We say that $s$ is \emph{integral over $R$} if there exists a monic polynomial with coefficients in $R$ that $s$ satisfies. In other words, $s$ is integral over $R$ if there are elements $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in R$ such that
\[
s^n+a_1s^{n-1}+a_2s^{n-2}+\cdots+a_{n-1}s+a_n=0
\]
in $S$, where each $a_i$ acts through the structure map $R\to S$. The word \emph{monic} is essential. For example, every rational number satisfies some nonzero polynomial over $\Z$, but only the integers satisfy monic polynomial equations over $\Z$. The monic condition is exactly what allows us to solve for high powers of $s$ in terms of lower powers.

\begin{problem}{9} \textbf{First Examples of Integral Elements.} 
    
    Let $S$ be an $R$-algebra.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Show that every element in the image of $R\to S$ is integral over $R$.
        \item Show that every nilpotent element of $S$ is integral over $R$.
        \item Let $S=R[x]/\langle x^2-r\rangle$ for some $r\in R$. Show that the image of $x$ in $S$ is integral over $R$.
        \item Show that $i\in \Z[i]$ is integral over $\Z$.
        \item Show that $t\in \K[t]$ is integral over $\K[t^2]$.
        \item Show that $\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{-3}+1)\in \Q(\sqrt{-3})$ is integral over $\Z[\sqrt{-3}]$. 
        \item Show directly that $1/2\in \Q$ is not integral over $\Z$. Hint: Suppose $(1/2)^n+a_1(1/2)^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n=0$ and clear denominators.   
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Every element $s \in \im(\alpha)$ in the image of $\alpha: R \to S$ is of the form $\alpha(r) = s \implies s - \alpha(r) = 0$. 
        
        \item Assume $s^n = 0$. Then we have, 
        \[s^n + 0 s^{n-1} + \cdots + 0 = 0\]
        Thus, every nilpotent element of $S$ is integral over $R$.

        \item We have, 
        \[\overline{x}^2 + 0 \overline{x} - r = 0\]
        Thus, the image of $x$ in $S$ is integral over $R$.

        \item We have, 
        \[i^2 + 0i + 1 = 0\]
        Thus $i \in \Z[i]$ is integral over $\Z$. 

        \item We have, 
        \[(t)^2 + 0(t)  - t^2 = 0\]
        Thus $t \in \K[t]$ is integral over $\K[t^2]$.
        
        \item We have, 
        \begin{align*}
            \left(\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{-3} + 1)\right)^2 - \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{-3} + 1) + 1 &= \frac{1}{4}\left(-2 + 2 \sqrt{-3}\right) - \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{-3} + 1)  + 1 \\ 
            &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{-3} - 1\right) - \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{-3} + 1)  + 1 \\ 
            &= \frac{-2}{2} + 1 = 0 
        \end{align*}
        Thus $\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{-3}+1)\in \Q(\sqrt{-3})$ is integral over $\Z[\sqrt{-3}]$.

        \item Suppose for the sake of contradiction that $\frac{1}{2}$ was integral over $\Z$. Then for some $n$ we would have, 
        
        \[\fr{1}{2}^n + a_{n-1} \fr{1}{2}^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 \fr{1}{2} + a_0 = 0\]
        Then, 
        \begin{align*}
           0 &= 2^n \left(\fr{1}{2}^n + a_{n-1} \fr{1}{2}^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 \fr{1}{2} + a_0\right) \\ 
           &= 1 + 2a_{n-1} + \cdots + 2^{n-1}a_1 + 2^n  \\ 
           \implies 1 &= 2 (a_{n-1} + \cdots + 2^{n-2}a_1 + 2^{n-1})
        \end{align*}
        But this is a contradiction. Hence $\frac{1}{2}$ is not integral over $\Z$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{10} \textbf{Integral Elements Give Finite Modules.} 
    
    Let $S$ be an $R$-algebra and let $s\in S$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Suppose that the element $s\in S$ is integral over $R$, and 
        \[
        s^n+a_1s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n=0
        \]
        with $a_i\in R$. Show that every power $s^m$ with $m\geq n$ is an $R$-linear combination of $1,s,\ldots,s^{n-1}$.
        \item Deduce that $R[s]$ is generated as an $R$-module by $1,s,\ldots,s^{n-1}$.
        \item Find a finite set of $R$-module generators for $S=\K[t]$ over $R=\K[t^2]$
        \item Find a finite set of $R$-module generators for $S=\Z[i]$ over $R=\Z$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item We will prove this by induction. 
        
        \textbf{Base Case:} $m = n$.

        Then we have, 
        \[s^n = - (a_1s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n)\]
        which is a $R$-linear combination of $1, s, \ldots, s^{n-1}$. 

        \textbf{Inductive Hypothesis:} Assume that $s^{m-1}$ can be written as a $R$-linear combination of $1, s, \ldots, s^{n-1}$. Let $s^{m-1} = \sum_{i = 0}^{n-1} r_i s^i$. 

        \textbf{Inductive Step:} Then, 
        \begin{align*}
            s^m &= s \cdot s^{m-1} = s \cdot \sum_{i = 0}^{n-1} r_i s^i  \\ 
            &= \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} r_{i-1} s^{i} + r_{n-1}s^n  \\ 
            &= \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} r_{i-1} s^{i} + r_{n-1} \left(- (a_1s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n)\right)
        \end{align*}
        Thus every power $s^m$ with $m \geq n$ is a $R$-linear combination of $1, s, \ldots, s^{n-1}$.

        \item An element of $R[s]$ are finite sums of the  form $\sum_i r_is^i $ with $r_i \in R$. Since $s^i$ with $i \geq n$ can be written as a $R$-linear combination of $1, s, \ldots, s^{n-1}$, $R[s]$ is spanned by $1, s, \ldots, s^{n-1}$ and hence is generated as a $R$-module by $1, s, \ldots, s^{n-1}$. 
        
        \item $\{1, t\}$ 
        
        \item $\{1, i\}$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{12}
    \textbf{Integral Elements Form a Subring.} 
    
    Let $R \to S$ be an $R$-algebra. Let $\overline{R}^{S}$ denote the set of elements of $S$ that are integral over $R$. When $R$ is an integral domain and $S$ is a field we call $\overline{R}^{S}$ the \emph{integral closure of $R$ in $S$}. The \emph{integral closure} or \emph{normalization} of a domain $R$ is the integral closure of $R$ in its field of fractions $S=\Frac(R)$. We denote the integral closure of $R$ by $\overline{R}$. 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If $x\in S$ is integral over $R$, prove that $R[x]$ is module-finite over $R$.

        \item If $x,y\in S$ are integral over $R$, prove that $R[x,y]$ is module-finite over $R$. Hint: First show $R[x]$ is finite over $R$, then show $R[x,y]$ is finite over $R[x]$.

        \item Let $s\in R[x,y]$. Show that multiplication by $s$ defines an
        $R$-linear map
        \[
        \mu_s:R[x,y]\longrightarrow R[x,y],
        \qquad
        f \longmapsto sf.
        \]
        Use Cayley--Hamilton, or the determinant trick, to prove that $s$ is
        integral over $R$.

        \item Conclude that $x+y$, $xy$, and $-x$ are integral over $R$.

        \item Conclude that $\overline{R}^{S}$ is a subring of $S$ containing the image of $R$.


    \end{enumerate}    
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item We know this from Problem 10. 
        
        \item We know that if $x \in S$ is integral over $R$ then, $R[x]$ is module finite over $R$. We also know that $y \in S$ is integral over $R$. That is there exist $a_i \in R$ such that, 
        \[y^n + a_1y^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0\]
        Note that since $a_i \in R$ they are in $R[x]$ and this equation also holds in $R[x]$. That is, $y$ is integral over $R[x]$, and $R[x][y]$ is module-finite over $R[x]$ and thus module-finite over $R$ (Problem 7, part 2). 

        \item By Cayley-Hamilton we have the characteristic polynomial, 
        \[p(\mu_s) = \mu_s^n + p_1\mu_s^{n-1} + \cdots + p_{n-1}\mu_s + p_n = 0\]
        with $p_k \in R$. Applying $\mu_s$ to $1 \in R[x,y]$ gives us
        \[p(s \cdot 1) = s^n + p_1s^{n-1} + \cdots + p_{n-1}s + p_n = 0\]
        which is a polynomial in $s$ with coefficients in $R$ and thus $s$ is integral over $R$. 

        \item Then $x+y, xy, -x \in R[x,y]$, and multiplication by each of these is an $R$-linear map on the finitely generated  $R$-module $R[x,y]$, so Cayley-Hamilton gives us a monic polynomial over $R$ and thus $x+y, xy, -x$ are integral over $R$. 
        
        \item Thus we conclude that for $\alpha: R \to S$, every $\alpha(r)$ is integral over $R$ and thus $ \im(R) \subseteq S$. Moreover, part 4 shows that $\overline{R}^S$ is closed under addition, multiplication and inverses and thus is a subring of $S$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{13}
    \textbf{Computations with Integrality.} 
    
    Let $\K$ be a field.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Prove that the elements of $\Q$ integral over $\Z$ are exactly the integers. Hint: Write a rational number in lowest terms.
        \item Show that $\K[t]$ is module-finite over $\K[t^2,t^3]$. Find explicit module generators.
        \item Show that $\K[x,y]/\langle y^2-x^3\rangle$ is module-finite over $\K[x]$. Find explicit module generators.
        \item Let $S=\K[x,x^{-1}]$, viewed as a $\K[x]$-algebra. Show that $x^{-1}$ is not integral over $\K[x]$.
        \item Deduce that $\K[x,x^{-1}]$ is algebra-finite but not module-finite over $\K[x]$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Assume for contradiction that some rational number $\frac{p}{q} \in \Q$, $q \neq 0$ is integral over $\Z$. Without loss of generality we assume $\gcd(p, q) = 1$. Then, there exists a polynomial with coefficients in $\Z$ as follows, 
        \[\fr{p}{q}^n + a_1\fr{p}{q}^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0\]
        Then, 
        \begin{align*}
            0 &= q^n \left(\fr{p}{q}^n + a_1\fr{p}{q}^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n\right) \\ 
            &= p^n + a_1 qp^{n-1} + \cdots + a_nq^n \\ 
        \implies p^n &= - q(a_1 p^{n-1} + \cdots + a_nq^{n-1})  \\
        \end{align*}
        That is $q \mid p^n$, but because $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ we have $\gcd(p^n, q) = 1$, so $q \mid 1 \implies q = \pm 1$. Thus, $\frac{p}{q} \in \Z$. Thus, the only elements of $\Q$ integral over $\Z$ are exactly the integers. 

        \item Since $\K[t]$ is module-finite over $\K[t^2]$ it is also module-finite  over $\K[t^2, t^3]$. The module generators are $\{1, t\}$. 
        
        \item Since $\overline{y}^2 = \overline{x}^3$, every element of $\K[x, y]/\langle y^2 - x^3 \rangle$ can be written as $p(x) + q(x)y$ for $p, q \in \K[x]$, so $\{1, \overline{y}\}$ generates it as a $\K[x]$ module. 
        
        \item Assume $x^{-1}$ is integral over $\K[x]$. Then, there exists a polynomial with coefficients in $\K[x]$ as follows, 
        \[\fr{1}{x}^n + a_1\fr{1}{x}^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0\]
         Then, 
        \begin{align*}
            0 &= x^n \left(\fr{1}{x}^n + a_1\fr{1}{x}^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n\right) \\ 
            &= 1 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_nx^n \\ 
        \implies 1 &= - x(a_1 + \cdots + a_n x^{n-1}) 
        \end{align*}
        Hence a contradiction. Thus, $x^{-1}$ is not integral over $\K[x]$. 

        \item Thus $\K[x, x^{-1}]$ is algebra-finite but not module-finite over $\K[x]$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

An $R$-algebra $S$ is called an \emph{integral extension} of $R$ if every element of $S$ is integral over $R$. Said differently, the structure map $R\to S$ is integral if all elements of the target satisfy monic polynomial equations over the source. Integral extensions are the precise setting where algebra generation and module generation interact especially well. The exercises below prove the fundamental comparison theorem:
\[
\boxed{\text{module-finite}}\quad \Longleftrightarrow\quad \boxed{\text{algebra-finite and integral}}.
\]
More explicitly, the exercises that follow will guide us through the proof of the following theorem. 

\begin{theorem}
Let $R\to S$ be an $R$-algebra.  The  $R$-algebra $S$ is module-finite over $R$ if and only if it $S$ is algebra-finite as an $R$-algebra. 
\end{theorem}

Note you will often, especially in algebraic geometry, see this expressed as finite is equivalent to finite type and integral. However, again we try to avoid this language for now.

\item \textbf{Finite Type Integral Extensions Are Module-Finite.} Suppose $S=R[s_1,\ldots,s_n]$ and each $s_i$ is integral over $R$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that $R[s_1]$ is module-finite over $R$.
    \item Prove that $R[s_1,s_2]$ is module-finite over $R[s_1]$, and hence module-finite over $R$.
    \item Continue by induction to prove that $S$ is module-finite over $R$.
    \item Deduce that if $S$ is algebra-finite and integral over $R$, then $S$ is module-finite over $R$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{The Comparison Theorem.} Let $S$ be an $R$-algebra. Prove that the following are equivalent.

    \item $S$ is module-finite over $R$.
    \item $S$ is algebra-finite over $R$ and integral over $R$.

\item \textbf{Permanence of Integral Extensions.} Let $R\to S\to T$ be maps of rings.
\begin{enumerate}

    \item Suppose $S$ is integral over $R$ and $T$ is integral over $S$. Prove that $T$ is integral over $R$. Hint: If $t\in T$ satisfies a monic equation over $S$, first adjoin to $R$ the finitely many coefficients appearing in that equation.
    \item If $S$ is integral over $R$ and $J\subseteq S$ is an ideal, prove that $S/J$ is integral over $R/(J\cap R)$.
    \item If $S$ is integral over $R$ and $U\subset R$ is multiplicatively closed, prove that $U^{-1}S$ is integral over $U^{-1}R$.
    \item More generally, if $S$ is integral over $R$ and $R\to R'$ is any ring map, prove that $R'\otimes_R S$ is integral over $R'$.
\end{enumerate}

    \item Deduce that if $\fq$ is maximal, then $\fp$ is maximal.
    \item Prove the converse: if $\fp$ is maximal, then $\fq$ is maximal. Hint: $S/\fq$ is an integral domain integral over the field $R/\fp$.
    \item Conclude that under an integral ring map, a prime ideal upstairs is maximal if and only if its contraction downstairs is maximal.

We now introduce a numerical invariant of a ring. A \emph{chain of prime ideals of length $d$} in a ring $R$ is a strictly increasing chain
\[
\fp_0\subsetneq \fp_1\subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq \fp_d
\]
of prime ideals. Thus the length counts the number of strict inclusions, not the number of prime ideals appearing in the chain. For a nonzero ring $R$, the \emph{Krull dimension} of $R$ is
\[
\dim(R)
\coloneqq
\sup\left\{d\in\Z_{\geq 0}\;\middle|\;\text{there exists a chain }\fp_0\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\fp_d\text{ in }\Spec(R)\right\}.
\]
If there are chains of arbitrarily large finite length, we write $\dim(R)=\infty$. The zero ring has no prime ideals; different authors use different conventions for its dimension, so we will avoid using the zero ring in basic dimension computations unless a convention is specified. We will eventually prove the following theorem, which will turn out to be key to computing the dimension of most rings.

\begin{theorem}
	If $\K$ is a field then $\dim \K[x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n]=n$.
\end{theorem}

Proving that the dimension of the polynomial ring $\K[x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n]$ is at least $n$ is relatively easy, all one must do is find an explicit chain of prime ideals of length $n$. You will do this in the exercises below. Proving the equality in the theorem requires us to prove the other inequality, i.e. we must show there is no chain of prime ideals of length more than $n$. This turns out to be surprisingly difficult. For the remainder of the worksheet you may assume this theorem unless it trivializes an exercise. 

You will also prove that if $R$ is any ring then $\dim R[x] \geq \dim R + 1$, again by exhibiting an explicit chain of prime ideals. Intuition tells us that this should be an equality, however, this turns out to be false unless we put additional hypotheses on $R$, namely that $R$ be Noetherian. This highlights a somewhat tedious fact about dimension theory in commutative algebra: unless one assumes some form of finiteness condition (Noetherian, finitely generated $\K$-algebra, integral, etc.) there are many pathologies. 

Using the correspondence between prime ideals and irreducible closed subsets of $\Spec(R$), thee intuition is that longer chains of prime ideals correspond to longer chains of irreducible closed subsets in $\Spec(R)$, ordered in the opposite direction.
