\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Nullstellensatz I] If $\K$ is algebraically closed, then every maximal ideal of $\K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is of the form $\langle x_1-a_1,\ldots,x_n-a_n\rangle$ for some point $(a_1,\ldots,a_n) \in \K^n$.
\end{theorem}  
 
 Note this is just the first of a series of theorems that will go by Hilbert's Nullstellensatz. Recall that a $\K$-algebra is a ring $R$ together with a specified ring homomorphism $\K \to R$. A morphism of $\K$-algebras $\varphi:R\to S$ is a ring homomorphism that preserves scalars, i.e.\ $\varphi(c\cdot 1_R)=c\cdot 1_S$ for every $c\in \K$. In particular, $\K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is a $\K$-algebra, every evaluation map $\ev_p:\K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\to \K$ is a $\K$-algebra homomorphism, and if $\fm$ is a maximal ideal then $R/\fm$ is naturally a field equipped with a $\K$-algebra structure. When we say below that a residue field is ``isomorphic to $\K$,'' we mean \emph{as a $\K$-algebra}.

\begin{problem}{3}
    \textbf{Defining Ideal of an Algebraic Set.} 
    
    Let $V \subset \K^n$ be an algebraic set. The \emph{defining ideal} of $V$ is the ideal in $\K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ given by 
    \[
    \I(V) \coloneqq \left\{ f \in \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n] \;\; \big| \;\; f(p) = 0 \text{ for all $p \in V$} \right\}.
    \]
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),ref=\theenumi\alph*]
        \item Prove that $\I(V)$ is in fact an ideal. 
        \item Prove that $\I(V)$ is radical. 
        \item If $V = \V(\cS)$ for some subset $\cS \subset \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, show that $\langle \cS \rangle \subset \sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle} \subset \I(V)$. 
        \item\label{ex-pt:big-obstruction} Let $\K=\R$. Give an example to show that the inclusion $\sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle } \subset \I(V)$ can be proper. 
        \item If $V = \V(\cS)$ for some subset $\cS \subset \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, show that $\V(\cS) = \V(\I(V))$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}


\begin{solution}
    \bbni 
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),ref=\theenumi\alph*]
        \item Let $g \in \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]$ and $f_1, f_2 \in \I(V)$. That is, $f_1(p) = f_2(p) = 0$. 
        
        \textbf{Subgroup Under Addition:} Note that, \[(f_1 + f_2)(p) = f_1(p) + f_2(p) = 0\] The  zero polynomial $0(p) = 0$ acts as the identity, and \[f_1(p) + (-f_1)(p) = 0(p) = 0\] gives us the additive inverse.  

        \textbf{Closed Under Multiplication:} Notice, 
        \[(g \cdot f)(p) = g(p) \cdot f_1(p) = g(p) \cdot 0 = 0\]
        Thus $g \cdot f \in \I(V)$ and $\I(V)$ is an ideal. 

        \item We want to show that $\I(V) = \sqrt{\I(V)}$. 

        Note that the containment $\I(V) \subseteq \sqrt{\I(V)}$ is obvious. To show the opposite containment take $f \in \sqrt{\I(V)}$. Then for some $n$ we have, 
        \[f^n(p) = (f(p))^n = 0\]
        But since $\K$ is a domain, this implies that $f(p) = 0$. Hence $f \in \I(V)$, $\I(V) \supseteq \sqrt{\I(V)}$, and $\I(V) = \sqrt{\I(V)}$.

        \item Let $V = \V(\cS)$. We know that $\langle \cS \rangle \subseteq \sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle}$. So showing that $\sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle} \subset \I(V)$ suffices. Note that 
        $\I(V) = \I(\V(\cS))$ which is the set of all functions that evaluate to $0$ at $p$, for all $p \in \V(\cS)$. And $\V(\cS)$ is the set of all points $p$ such that all functions $f \in \cS$ evaluate to $0$ at $p$. Let $f \in \sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle}$. Then by definition for some $n$, $f^n in \S$. We can then write, 
        \[f^n = \sum_ih_i(p)g_i(p), \quad h_i \in \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n], g_i \in \cS\]
        But then, 
        \[f^n(p) = \sum_ih_i(p)g_i(p) = \sum_ih_i(p)\cdot 0 = 0\]
        Moreover since $\K$ is a domain, $f^n(p) = 0 \implies f(p) = 0 \implies f$ is a function that evaluates to $0$ at $p$, hence $f \in \I(V)$. 

        \item Consider the set $\cS = \{x^2+ 1\}$. We have, 
        \[\sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle} = \sqrt{\langle x^2 + 1 \rangle}\]
        And, because the polynomial has no solutions over $\R$, we have $V = \V(\cS) = \emptyset$. But $\I(\V(\cS)) = \R[x]$. Also note that since $\langle \cS \rangle$ is a prime ideal ($x^2 +1$ is irreducible), we have $ {\langle x^2 + 1 \rangle} = \sqrt{\langle x^2 + 1 \rangle} \subset \R[x]$. Hence, the containment $\sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle} \subset \I(V)$ can be proper. 

        \item Let $V =\V(\cS)$. 
        
        $\V(\cS) \subseteq \V(\I(\V(\cS)))$

        This containment is obvious by construction. $\I(\V(\cS))$ is the set of all functions that vanish at all points in $\V(\cS)$. And $\V(\I(\V(\cS)))$ is the set of all points that all functions in $\I(\V(\cS))$ vanish at. So, $\V(\cS) \subseteq \V(\I(V))$. 

        \phantom{A}

        $\V(\cS) \supseteq \V(\I(\V(\cS)))$

        Note that by definition $\cS \subseteq \I(\V(\cS))$. Then by 2(a) $\V(\I(\V(\cS))) \subseteq \V(\cS)$.
        
        Hence, $\V(\cS) = \V(\I(\V(\cS)))$.
    \end{enumerate}    
\end{solution}

So far you have built two order-reversing operations:
\[
\cS \longmapsto \V(\cS)
\quad\text{and}\quad
V \longmapsto \I(V).
\]
You have shown that passing from equations to a zero set only remembers the radical of the ideal they generate, and Question~\ref{ex-pt:big-obstruction} shows that over a non-algebraically closed field this can lose information. The next theorem says that over an algebraically closed field this is the only ambiguity.
 
\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Nullstellensatz II] If $\K$ is algebraically closed and $V = \V(\cS) \subset \K^n$ is an algebraic set, for some subset $\cS \subset \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, then $\I(V) = \sqrt{\langle \cS \rangle}$. 
\end{theorem}  

At this point the basic affine dictionary is taking shape; Question~(4) asks you to make the correspondence between algebraic sets and radical ideals completely explicit. After that we turn to coordinate rings, which package an algebraic set $V$ into algebra and will let us recover from $\K[V]$ its points, its topology, and ultimately its morphisms.

\begin{problem}{5}
    \textbf{Coordinate Rings.} 
    
    Let $V \subset \K^n$ be an algebraic set and $\cR = \Hom_{\text{Set}}(V,\K)$ be the ring of all (set) functions from $V$ to $\K$. In this question we study the restriction map from $\K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ to $\cR$ given by:
    \[
    \begin{tikzcd}[column sep = 3.5em, row sep = .5em]
    \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n] \arrow[r, "\rho"] & \cR\\
    f \arrow[r, mapsto] & f|_{V}
    \end{tikzcd}.
    \]
    Here we are viewing $f \in \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ as defining a function $\K^n \to \K$ given by $p \mapsto f(p)$. We call the image of $\rho$ the \emph{coordinate ring} of $V$ and denote it $\K[V]$.

            \item Let $V = \V(xy-1) \subset \K^{2}$ and consider the function $V\to \K$ given by $(x,y) \mapsto \frac{1}{x}$.

        \item Let $f_1, f_2 \in \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]$. Note that addition and multiplication is defined pointwise in $R$. To see if $\rho$ is a ring homomorphism, we check if the three properties hold: 

        \textbf{Addition: }
        \[\rho(f_1) + \rho(f_2)(p) = f_1|_V(p) + f_2|_V(p) = (f_1+f_2)|_V(p) = \rho(f_1+f_2)(p)\]


        \textbf{Multiplication: }
        \[\rho(f_1) \cdot \rho(f_2)(p) = f_1|_V(p) \cdot f_2|_V(p) = (f_1\cdot f_2)|_V(p) = \rho(f_1\cdot f_2)(p)\]

        \textbf{Unital:}
        \[\rho(1)(p) = 1|_V(p) = 1\]

	    \item 
        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), ref=\theenumi\roman*]
            \item Let $f: (x,y) \mapsto e^{x^2 + y^2}$. Note that on $V$, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ then, $f|_V = e^{x^2+y^2} = e^1$. Which behaves like the constant polynomial function $g(x,y) = e$ and hence lies in the ring of coordinates of $V$. 
            \item Let $f: (x,y) \mapsto \frac{1}{x}$. Note that on $V$, $\frac{1}{x}= y$, and $x \neq 0$ because $\K$ is a domain. Then, $f|_V = \frac{1}{x} = y$. Which behaves like the polynomial function $g(x,y) = y$ and hence lies in the ring of coordinates of $V$. 
        \end{enumerate}

        \item The kernel of $\rho$ is $\I(V)$. 
        \[\K[V] \iso \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n] / \I(V)\]

        \item The coordinate ring of $\K^n$, $\K[\K^n]$ is the ring of all set functions $\K^n \to \K$. 
        \[e_i^\vee (x_1, \cdots, x_n) = x_i\]
        which corresponds to the polynomial function $g(x_1, \cdots, x_n) = x_i$. Hence, $e_i^\vee \in \K[\K^n]$. 

        Under the presentation given in part (c) we have, 
        \[\K[\K^n] \iso \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n] / \I(\K^n) = \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n] / \{0\} = \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n] \]
        So, $e_i^\vee$ corresponds to the coset $[x_i] = x_i$. 

        \item $\K[V]$ is the coordinate ring of $V$ because every polynomial is a combination of coordinate functions restricted to $V$. And $\K[V]$ is generated by these functions on $V$. 
        \[\K[V] = \K[e_1^\vee|_V, \cdots, e_n^\vee |_V]\]
        
        \item 
        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), ref=\theenumi\roman*]
            \item Let $V = \V(y-x^2)$. 
            
            \[\K[V] \iso \K[x,y]/\I(y - x^2) = \K[x, x^2] = \K[x]\]

            \item Let $V = \V(y^2, xy, x^{17})$. 
            
            Note that $\I(V) = \sqrt{\langle y^2, xy, x^{17} \rangle }$. 
            
            Since $\K$ is a field, if $(a, b) \in V$ then
            
            \[a^{17} = 0 \implies a = 0\]
            and 
            \[b^2 = 0 \implies b = 0\]

            Then, $V = \{(0, 0)\}$. And $I(V) = I (\{(0,0)\}) = \langle x, y \rangle$. 


            \[\K[V] \iso \K[x,y]/\I(\V(y^2, xy, x^{17})) = \K[x,y] / \langle x, y \rangle = \K\]

            \item Let $V = \V(x^6 - 3x^5y + 3x^4y^2 + x^3y^3) = \V(x^3(x-y)^3)$. 
            
            Then if $(a, b) \in V$, either $a^3 = 0 \implies a = 0$ or $(x-y)^3 = 0 \implies x = y$. 
            
            Then $V = \{(0, a), (a, a) \, | \, a \in \K\} = \V(x) \cup \V(x-y)$. 
            \[\I(V) = \I(\V(x) \cup \V(x-y)) = \I(\V(x)) \cap \I(\V(x-y)) = \langle x \rangle \cap \langle x-y \rangle = \langle x(x-y) \rangle \]
            And 
            \[\K[V] \iso \K[x,y]/\langle x(x-y) \rangle \]
            
            \item Let $A = (x_{ij}) \in SL_3(\F_5)$, and $V = \V(\det(A) - 1, \, tr(A))$. Note that the trace formula is $x_{11} + x_{22} + x_{33}$, and we use fermat's little theorem to enforce staying in $\F_5$. Then the coordinate ring is, 
            \[\K[V] = \F_5[x_{11} , \cdots, x_{33}] / \langle \det(A) - 1, x_{11} + x_{22} + x_{33}, x_{ij}^5 - x_{ij} \rangle\]
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\hrulefill

You should now think of $\K[V]$ as the algebraic avatar of $V$: its elements are precisely the polynomial functions on $V$, and the coordinate functions on $\K^n$ descend to generators of $\K[V]$. The next step is to recognize points of $V$ purely algebraically, either as $\K$-algebra maps $\K[V]\to \K$ or as maximal ideals whose residue fields are isomorphic to $\K$ as $\K$-algebras.

\hrulefill

\begin{problem}{6}
    Let $R$ be a finitely generated $\K$-algebra. Fix a presentation $R \cong \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I$. Describe a natural bijection between the following three sets:
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), ref=\theenumi\roman*]
 	\item points in $\V(I) \subset \K^n$, 
	\item maximal ideals in $R$ with residue field isomorphic to $\K$ as a $\K$-algebra, and 
	\item elements of $\Hom_{\K-\alg}(R,\K)$, the set of morphisms from $R$ to $\K$ in the category of $\K$-algebras. 
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    
    Let $\K$ be algebraically closed. 

    \textbf{Claim:} Let $\pi: \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n] \to \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]/I = R$, and let $J \subset R$ be maximal. We claim that $\pi^{-1}(J)$ is maximal in $\K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]$. 

    \begin{proof}
        We know that $\pi^{-1}(J)$ is an ideal. Let $M$ be a maximal ideal containing $\pi^{-1}(J)$. Since $\pi$ is surjective, we know that $\pi(M)$ is an ideal (worksheet $1.1, Q4.4$), and $J \subseteq \pi(M)$. Then, since $J$ is maximal, either $\pi(M) = J$ or $\pi(M)$ is the whole ring. $\pi(M) \neq R$ since ring homomorphisms are unital and $1 \notin M$. Hence, $M = \pi^{-1}(J)$ or $\pi^{-1}(J)$ is maximal. 
    \end{proof}

    
    $(i) \iff (ii)$

    Recall that $\V(I)$ contains points $p = (a_1, \cdots, a_n)$ such that $f(p) = 0$ for all $f \in I$. Also, 
    \[\ev_p: \K^n \to \K\]

    By Hilbert's Nullstellensatz I, we know that maximal ideals of $\K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]$ are of the form $\langle x_1 - a_1, \cdots, x_n -a_n \rangle$, for some point $(a_1, \cdots, a_n)$. 
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}{7}
    \textbf{Zariski Topology on Algebraic Sets.} Let $V \subseteq \K^n$ be an algebraic set. Define the \emph{Zariski topology} on $V$ by declaring the closed sets to be the subsets of $V$ of the form $\V(I)$ for some ideal $I \subset \K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$. In other words, the closed sets are precisely the \emph{algebraic subsets} of $V$.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),ref=\theenumi\alph*]
        \item Verify that this definition indeed determines a topology on $V$.

        \item For $f \in \K[V]$, define
        \[
        D(f) = \left\{p \in V \;\; \big| \;\;  f(p) \neq 0\right \}.
        \]
        Show that $\{D(f)\}$ form a basis for the Zariski topology on $V$.

        \item For each point $p \in V$, let
        \[
        \fm_p = \left\{f \in \K[V] \;\; \big| \;\;  f(p)=0 \right\}
        \]
        be the corresponding maximal ideal. Show that the map
        \[
        \begin{tikzcd}[row sep = .5em, column sep = 3.5em]
        V \arrow[r] & \Spec(\K[V]) \\
        p \arrow[r,mapsto] & \fm_p
        \end{tikzcd}
        \]
        is a homeomorphism onto its image, which consists of those maximal ideals whose residue field is isomorphic to $\K$ as a $\K$-algebra.
        \item If $\K$ is algebraically closed how can the preceding part be rephrased in terms of $\mSpec(\K[V])$?
        \item Is the Zariski topology on $V$ Hausdorff in general? If not, when is it? 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    \bbni
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),ref=\theenumi\alph*]
        \item $V = \V(I)$ for some $I \subset \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]$. The closed sets of $V$ are of the form $\V(J)$, where $I \subset J \subset \K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]$. We need to check the following: 
        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),ref=\theenumi\roman*]
        \item $\emptyset$ and $V$ are closed. 
        
        Note $\V(I) = V$, and $\V(\K[x_1, \cdots, x_n]) = \emptyset$ is closed. 

        \item Finite union of closed sets is closed.
        
        Let $\V(J), \V(K) \subseteq V$ be subsets. Then, $\V(I) \cup \V(J)$ is the set of points in $\K^n$ such that for all $p \in \V(I) \cup \V(J)$ either $f(p) = 0$ or $g(p) = 0$. 
        \item Arbitrary intersection of closed sets is closed. 
    \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\hrulefill

By now you have recovered from $\K[V]$ not just the points of $V$, but also its Zariski topology. The next goal is to understand maps in the same way: a morphism of algebraic sets should be encoded contravariantly by a $\K$-algebra homomorphism of coordinate rings.

\hrulefill

\begin{problem}{8}
    \textbf{Morphisms of Algebraic Sets.} A \emph{polynomial map} from $\K^n$ to $\K^m$ is a function of the form: 
    \[
    \Phi: \K^n \longrightarrow \K^m \quad p \mapsto (f_1(p), \dots, f_{m}(p)).
    \]
    where  $f_1, \dots, f_m \in \K[x_1,\dots,x_n]$.  A \emph{morphism of algebraic sets} $V \to W$ is defined to be the restriction of such a polynomial map $\Phi$ with $\Phi(V) \subset W$. Fix a polynomial map $\Phi$ as above. 
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),ref=\theenumi\alph*]
	\item Show that there is an induced $\K$-algebra homomorphism
	\[
	\phi: \K[\K^{m}] \longrightarrow \K[\K^{n}] \quad \text{given by} \quad  g \mapsto g \circ \Phi.
	\]
	Describe $\phi$ explicitly in terms of the coordinate function generators of $\K[\K^{m}]$.
	\item Let $V \subset \K^n$ and $W \subset \K^m$ be algebraic sets. Show that $\Phi(V) \subset W$ if and only if $\phi(\I(W)) \subset \I(V)$.
	\item Deduce that $\Phi$ restricts to a morphism of algebraic sets $\Phi|_V : V \longrightarrow W$ if and only if the composition
	\[
	\begin{tikzcd}[row sep = .3em, column sep = 3.5em]
	\K[\K^m] \arrow[r, "\phi"] & \K[\K^n] \arrow[r, "\rho"] &\K[V]
	\end{tikzcd}
	\]
	factors through $\K[W]$. Here $\rho$ is the restriction map.
	\item For each of the given maps of coordinate rings, describe the corresponding map of algebraic sets. When possible draw pictures. Compare to the corresponding maps on spectra.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), ref=\theenumi\roman*]
		\item $\CC[x,y] \rightarrow \CC[t]$ given by $x\mapsto t$ and $y\mapsto t$.
		\item $\CC[t] \rightarrow \CC[x,y]$ given by $t \mapsto x$.
		\item $\CC[t] \rightarrow \CC[x,y,t]/\langle xy-t \rangle$ given by $t \mapsto t$. 
	\end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\hrulefill

You now have the main algebra-geometry dictionary in hand. Over any field $\K$, every algebraic set $V \subset \K^n$ has a coordinate ring $\K[V]$, points of $V$ are the same as $\K$-algebra maps $\K[V]\to \K$, and morphisms $V \to W$ correspond contravariantly to $\K$-algebra homomorphisms $\K[W]\to \K[V]$. Thus the assignment
\[
V \longmapsto \K[V]
\]
always gives a contravariant bridge from geometry to algebra. When $\K$ is algebraically closed, Hilbert's Nullstellensatz says that every finitely generated reduced $\K$-algebra comes from an algebraic set, so this bridge becomes an anti-equivalence of categories.

Conclude that the assignment $V\mapsto \K[V]$ defines a  (anti-) equivalence from the category of algebraic sets over $\K$ to the category of finitely generated reduced $\K$-algebras.
